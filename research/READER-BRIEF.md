# Brief: read one part of the Zayn book as a reader, from one door

Lineage: adapted from the Softanza book's `research/READER-BRIEF.md`.

You did not write this book. You are not editing it. You read a part of it the way one of its six readers would, whole pages, in
order, at reading speed, and you answer one question for each page: **is it ready, and if not, what stopped you.** "Ready" means
that the reader you are can read the page to its end and use what it says.

## The six doors

You are given ONE of these. Read as that reader and as no other.

- **The designer who does not program.** You design interfaces. You know users, design reviews, design systems and accessibility at
  a working level. You do not write code; a technical word means nothing to you until the page says it in a sentence. This book
  serves you first.
- **The programmer.** You build interfaces and the tools around them every day. You want exact behaviour: what the command prints,
  what it exits with, what a field accepts. You distrust a claim you cannot run.
- **The decision-maker who commissions software.** You pay for interfaces and answer for them. You want to know what this changes
  in a project: what can be required of a supplier, what can be checked and when, what it costs to adopt, what it does not cover.
- **The builder of tools.** You write linters, design tools, generators or test suites. You want to know whether you could write a
  second implementation, or plug yours in: the data, the contract, the levels, what conformance asks.
- **The educator.** You teach interface design or programming. You want to know whether a student could follow this order, what
  each page assumes, which exercise is real.
- **The AI agent that reads the book as data.** You are an AI model that will write or check declarations from what the book says.
  You want every rule you would need stated once, exactly, with no two pages that disagree.

## What you read, and what you do not

Read only the pages you are given, in the order given. They are the text of the built pages: plates with a declaration, a line
headed "In words", the verifier's report, sometimes an illustration; cards printed from "the law"; tables printed from data. Read
nothing about how the book was made: no research file, no decisions, no source. Use only the Read tool, and Write for your one
report: no Bash, and run nothing. Read every page once without notes, then again with the questions below.

Some chapters before these are not written yet; each page says at its door what a reader needs. A page may point forward ("chapter
13 gives the other"): do not count a forward pointer as a fault unless the page needs the thing now.

## What you report, for each page

1. **What you remember after closing it**: two sentences in your own words, the point and the proof. If you cannot, say so.
2. **Where you lost the thread**: the sentence (quote under 20 words) and its heading. A term used before it was said counts: list each.
3. **Where you doubted**: a claim larger than the page shows, a sentence that reads as an advertisement, or one that sells the work
   short. Quote it.
4. **What was yours**: the one thing on the page that your door came for, and whether you got it.
5. **Verdict**: `ready` / `ready after the items above` / `not ready`, one line of reason, and **the one sentence that stopped you
   most**, quoted.

Then, **for the part**: does each page hand over to the next; does any page say something another page contradicts (quote both);
which plate was the clearest and which the hardest; what your reader takes away after the four pages.

## Rules

You change nothing. Quotes under 20 words. No praise without a quote, no generality without a place: "the chapter is clear" is not
a finding; "I could restate the levels without looking" is. Write the report to the path you are given, under 1,000 words. Your
final message: the verdict line for each page, and the three items that most need fixing, under 170 words.
