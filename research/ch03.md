## 1. What the chapter does

It reads what the field of human-computer interaction wrote about interfaces: Norman's gulfs of execution and evaluation,
Nielsen's heuristics, Shneiderman's golden rules, Fitts's and Hick's laws, the Gestalt principles, Tufte. For each piece it
shows where Zayn's law keeps it: as a numbered rule with an article and a tier, and, where a diagnostic binds that rule, as a
refusal the verifier prints before anything is drawn. It runs three of those refusals (visibility of system status, user
control and the way back, error prevention) and one on a closed vocabulary (consistency of status meaning), each offending
version before its lawful twin. It then says which pieces stay with a person's judgment, and why: a declaration names no
rendering, so the size of a target, the number of items in a menu, the grouping on a page and the ink of a chart are read on
the painted page or by a person. Chapter 1 already opened on the field's four kinds of instrument; this chapter is about the
knowledge, chapter 4 about the instruments, chapter 5 about the road sign.

error-prevention heuristics to Rules 79, 26 and 51 (`site/practices.html:86`), and Nielsen's "user control and freedom" to
`MISSING_UNDO` (`doc/design/SITE-2.md:106-107`); and Rule 50 to Tufte, by name.

## 4. The principle

The sentence the record returns to: "The heuristic judges what only a person can judge, and the law refuses what a machine
can see before a pixel exists." (`site/how.html:109`). Beside it: "Someone has to write the principles down in a form a
machine can refuse to violate." (`README.md:15`).

Proposed bold sentence, in the book's words: **The field wrote what a good interface does; the law keeps the part a
declaration can state as a rule that refuses, and leaves the rest to the eye that judges it.**

A shorter alternative: **Advice says what to do; a rule kept as data says no.** (Chapter 1's principle is "A remark corrects
one page, once; a rule kept as data is checked on every declaration an instrument reads"; the writer should keep the two
apart.)
